Evidence of systematic censorship

What is still denied

Europe's wealth also came from here: from the silver, from the land and from the labor of an entire people. In Mexico it is taught; elsewhere it is almost always told as an encounter, or not told at all. What is still denied is that it was plunder.

  • Mexico changed Europe and Western culture. Its silver sustained the Spanish empire [1]. Its crops changed the Old World's table and the diet of Europe, the Middle East and even East Asia [2]: the tomato of Italy and Greece, Hungary's paprika, the chile in kimchi and in Sichuan.
  • Europe does not know it. In Spain it is taught as an «encounter» [3]; in the rest of Europe it is barely told.
  • Not even Mexico grasps the scale of the genocide. In central Mexico, of every hundred people, three remained [4]. The SEP calls it «a significant decline» [5].

The city

1519

Before the silver there was a city on the water, and a web of tribute that reached the coasts.

In 1519, Tenochtitlan was a city of causeways, canals and chinampas. Together with Tlatelolco it had between some 212,500 and 400,000 people, depending on the estimate: one of the largest cities in the world [6]. The chinampas supplied much of its food [7].

Soldiers of Cortés who had been to Constantinople, Italy and Rome said they had never seen a market so large, so orderly and so full of people as the one in Tlatelolco [8].

Tribute from hundreds of towns flowed into the city: cotton cloaks, feathers, cacao, greenstone, gold. Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza had that record painted, province by province, to learn about the newly conquered land [9]. That web is what the Spaniards found. They did not dismantle it: they inherited it and squeezed it dry.

The fall

1520–1521

The city fell to war, siege and hunger. The first thing they took was the gold.

At the feast of Toxcatl, in May 1520, Spaniards led by Pedro de Alvarado entered the temple courtyard and attacked the unarmed people who were dancing [10, 11]. Cortés does not mention it in his letter to the king [11].

The siege came the following year. To advance, Cortés ordered the city's houses torn down and burned [12]. Friar Toribio de Motolinía wrote that the captains «leveled everything they won of the city», and compared the dead to those of Jerusalem when Titus destroyed it [13]. The siege ended on 13 August 1521 with the capture of Cuauhtémoc [10, 8].

Then came hunger: in the middle of the war no one could plant, and what one side planted the other destroyed [13].

And the gold. To make him say where it was, they burned Cuauhtémoc's feet [8]. Moctezuma's gold and silver discs traveled as a gift to Charles V [8]. The gold bar found in 1981 beneath the Bank of Mexico matches the gold the Spaniards melted down and lost while fleeing on the Noche Triste [14].

The plague

1520–1625

The epidemics did not reach a people at peace: they came on top of war, hunger and forced labor.

Smallpox arrived in 1520 with Narváez's men. In most provinces more than half the people died; in many houses everyone died, and since there was no one to bury them, the house was pulled down on top of them [13].

Others followed. The cocoliztli epidemics of 1545 and 1576 killed, by the estimates, between 5 and 15 million people [15]. And while millions of Indigenous people were dying, those who remained were made to give service and tribute without limit [16].

Cook and Borah estimated 25.2 million people in central Mexico in 1518 and about 730,000 by 1620–1625: around 3% [4]. Other authors start from fewer people, but all of them find the collapse [17].

The tribute

1522–1556

Once the war was won, the land was handed out along with its people. The towns paid with their labor, their goods and their children.

Under the encomienda, each town was bound to give tribute and labor to a colonist [18]. The first encomendero of Tepetlaoztoc, east of Lake Texcoco, was Hernán Cortés himself [19].

From 1522 to 1556, Tepetlaoztoc handed over gold jewels, obsidian mirrors, a gold box set with emeralds, beans, flour, chickens and porters. The demands and punishments killed so many people that the population fell within a few decades. The town painted all of it in a codex and took its encomenderos to court [19].

The tribute was so relentless that, to meet it, people sold their children and their land; many of those who could not pay died under torture or in cruel prisons [13]. The calpixques who collected it ordered the towns' lords around as if they were their slaves [13].

Spanish Mexico City was raised with that labor: Indigenous people supplied the materials, carried the beams and stones on their backs, and if they brought nothing to eat, they went hungry. Many died on the building sites [13]. The stones of the Templo Mayor were reused in Spanish buildings [20].

The mines

16th–18th centuries

The wealth that crossed the sea came out of the ground at the cost of lives. The whole Indigenous people bore that labor.

From the first years they went looking for mines, and the Indigenous slaves who died in them, Motolinía wrote, «could not be counted» [13].

To fill them, slaves were made in a hurry: «herds as large as flocks of sheep» entered Mexico City to be branded with iron. When there were not enough, they took the children and the common people; and since every owner who bought them added his own mark, some had their faces covered in letters [13].

The towns carried food on their backs to the mines from sixty leagues away and more. Many ran out of food and died there or on the way home, and the stench of the dead emptied whole towns [13].

In Mexico, slavery and forced labor fell on the whole Indigenous people: branded slaves, porters, encomienda tributaries. They worked until they died. Indigenous people were enslaved before and after the laws that banned it [21]; across the Americas, between 2.5 and 5 million [22]. And between 1580 and 1650, between 200,000 and 250,000 enslaved Africans were brought by force to New Spain [23].

From 1554 on, silver was separated with mercury in the patio process. The mines of Spanish America lost an average of 612 metric tons of mercury a year between 1580 and 1900 [24].

Where it went

16th–19th centuries

The silver left through Veracruz for Seville and through Acapulco for Manila. It almost never stayed in Spain.

Pick each point on the map to follow the silver.

A study of six European economies finds that when the arrival of American metals rose, the output of those economies rose six to nine years later [18].

What the Crown collected

1765–1810

Besides the silver, the king charged for almost everything: tobacco, pulque, salt, whatever was sold, and even the snow on the volcanoes. And part of it went straight to Madrid.

Around 1803, the Crown collected some 20 million pesos a year in New Spain [25]. The gold and silver mines brought it some 5.5 million; tobacco, 4 to 4.5 million; the alcabalas, almost 3 million; the tribute of the Indigenous people, 1.3 million; and pulque, some 800,000 [25].

Tobacco was the richest monopoly. Its sales rose from 1.5 million pesos in 1765 to more than 8 million a year around 1800, and its net product was set aside to be sent to Spain or to Cuba [1]. Around 1790, the monopoly employed 17,256 people [1].

Pulque was perhaps the most heavily taxed good: it carried a surcharge of close to 200% over its cost [1], and it was paid mostly by the Indigenous neighborhoods of the cities [1]. Even salt, gunpowder, playing cards, snow and cockfights were royal monopolies [1]. An Indigenous man who climbed the volcanoes for snow could not sell it without paying the king [25].

Each Indigenous head of household paid some two pesos of tribute a year [1]. And after 1795, the Royal Treasury systematically plundered the community chests of the Indigenous towns [1]. In the 1780s, a free worker possibly paid up to 20% of what he earned in taxes [1].

Where did it go? Of the 20 million, 10.5 were spent inside the viceroyalty, 3.5 were sent to other colonies and 6 went into the king's treasury in Madrid [25]. In 1792, what was sent abroad came to 9,354,334 pesos: 47% of everything collected. Between 1795 and 1799 it passed 11 million a year [1]. Between 1805 and 1808, the Consolidation of Royal Bonds raised more than 10 million pesos in New Spain, and most of it ended up paying debts to Napoleon [1].

The silver, and everything else

1521–today

Gold was the first thing seen. What drove three centuries was silver, and with it went everything else: cochineal, tobacco, vanilla. Many of those businesses live on today in other hands.

Between 1500 and 1800, Spanish America produced between 85,000 and 150,000 metric tons of silver: more than the rest of the world combined [18].

Humboldt wrote in 1803 that «more than two thousand million pesos fuertes» had come out of the Mexico City Mint in under 300 years [1]. That single figure, turned into today's silver, gives the count below.

Everything else left with the silver. In years of peace, some 17 million pesos in gold and silver left through Veracruz each year, along with 2.4 million in cochineal and 1.3 million in sugar, plus flour, indigo, hides, sarsaparilla, vanilla, jalap, soap and logwood [25]. The sugarcane came from abroad; the land it was planted in was from here.

Among Cortés's first shipments to Charles V were cloaks, bedspreads and tapestries of cotton woven here [26].

Even the maguey became someone else's business. During the colonial era, the only legal alcoholic drinks were the wines and brandies brought from Spain, and pulque [27]. Mezcal was banned in most of New Spain for three centuries; in Guadalajara, where it was allowed from the mid-seventeenth century on, it was taxed through a monopoly [27]. Tequila comes from that mezcal wine. And planting vineyards was forbidden, to protect the empire's trade [27].

And the plants the world now thinks are its own: maize, the tomato, chile, cacao, vanilla. Those left no account in pesos: the crops of the Americas changed the diet and the population of the Old World [2].

And there were businesses that did leave an account, in other hands.

In Spain, the tobacco revenue came to almost a third of domestic public income; in the Americas, the monopolies together were the Crown's second source of revenue, after gold and silver [28]. In 1767, Julián de Arriaga called it “the most flourishing the King has in Spain”; in 1794, Joaquín Maniau called the one here “the precious jewel the King has in his dominions of America” [29]. Seville raised for that business the largest industrial building in eighteenth-century Europe [30]. Today it is the university.

The plant behind that business is not Mexican: almost all commercial tobacco is a South American hybrid [31]. And Seville worked mainly with Cuban leaf. But the money to pay for it was drawn from New Spain [29]. In 1765, the king also took over the tobacco here: the monopoly shrank the growing areas and shut down a good number of cigar shops [29]. In 1796 it took in 7.3 million pesos and spent 3.4 [29]. From that Spanish monopoly of 1636 came Tabacalera, which held the monopoly until 1999 and that year merged with France's Seita into Altadis [32]. In 2007, Britain's Imperial Tobacco agreed to buy Altadis for 16.2 billion euros, debt included [33].

Dyes took another road. For three hundred years, cochineal was Mexico's most important export after silver, and it dyed the fine cloth of popes, princes and nobles all over Europe [34]. It reached luxury workshops such as those of Florence through Seville and Cádiz, and its price quadrupled during the sixteenth century [34]. Indigenous peasants in Oaxaca raised it. Under the repartimiento, district magistrates lent them money and collected in cochineal at a fixed price, below the international one; historians debate how much was coercion and how much incentive [34]. After Independence it was grown on a large scale in Guatemala and the Canary Islands, where it became the leading export [34].

With Campeche logwood, the plunder was literal. Europe's dyers wanted it for their blacks and dark blues [35]. In 1597, the English captain William Parker raided the coast of Yucatán and took logwood as spoils [35]. After 1670, former privateers stayed to cut it at Laguna de Términos and in Belize. At times they enslaved Maya people, and later brought enslaved Africans from Jamaica [35]. By 1750, Belize was exporting 8,000 tons, and the Netherlands and England already had mills to process it [35].

Other businesses began by carrying off the plant. Outside Mexico, vanilla bore almost no fruit [36]. In 1841, on the French island of Bourbon, now Réunion, Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy of twelve, pollinated it by hand [36]. The island went from 2 tons in 1858 to 200 in 1898 and overtook Mexico as the world's top producer. In 2017, Madagascar produced 40% and Mexico about 6% [36].

Sisal, an agave domesticated in Mexico, reached German East Africa in 1893 with the botanist Richard Hindorf, by way of Florida: the region's production grew from 62 plants. The plantations were worked by people forced to pay off colonial tax debts [37].

And Canada. Before the combine, hundreds of thousands of farmers in the United States and Canada bound their grain with twine, and the best came from the henequen and sisal of Yucatán [38]. To meet US demand, Yucatán's hacienda owners used debt to attract and bond their peons [39]. And with the deportation of the Yaqui from Sonora, ordered under Porfirio Díaz, Yucatán's businessmen filled their labor shortage [40].

In the early twentieth century, Canadian-based companies dominated Mexico's public utilities [41]. Mexican Light and Power, Mexico Tramways and the Mexico North Western Railway held long, sometimes perpetual concessions from Porfirio Díaz. Canada mostly supplied the intermediation: the securities were sold in Britain, Europe and the United States [42]. In 1960 Mexico bought the great majority of Mexican Light and Power's shares, and that year it reserved electricity for the nation [43].

Today the account goes on in the mines. Between 2006 and March 2024, Mexico's Ministry of Economy counted 415 Canadian-capital companies in Mexican mining [44], and Canada is the sector's main investor: 16.511 billion dollars between 1999 and 2024 [45]. In 2021, companies with foreign investment, from all countries, produced about 56% of the country's silver and 72% of its gold [46]. In 2022, a Vancouver company bought the Valenciana mine in Guanajuato, which by its account once yielded up to a third of the world's silver [47].

Concessions are today's embezzlement, with the same colonial logic. Between 2008 and 2013, what the State collected in mining duties was 0.6% of the value extracted: 60 centavos of every 100 pesos [48]. In 2009 Mariano Abarca was killed in Chicomuselo, Chiapas. He led the opposition to a barite mine run by Canada's Blackfire. By 2018 no one had been punished. His family points to the company and holds the Canadian embassy to account; these are accusations, not a ruling [49]. In 2022, the Supreme Court voided two Almaden concessions in Tecoltemi, Puebla, because no one had consulted the community [50]. The Canadian company took Mexico to international arbitration: in May 2026 it was claiming 2.68 billion dollars [51].

The road repeats itself. What is worth something leaves; what costs something stays here.

What was erased and is still denied

1536–today

Taking the wealth was not enough. Books were burned, peoples were herded together, names were changed and the lake was drained. And it is still denied: in Spain it is usually told as an encounter, and in the rest of the world it is hardly told at all.

First they persecuted those who kept the knowledge, then what was written. The timeline at the end of this act gives the dates. The smallpox plate you saw above comes from Sahagún's work: the one Philip II ordered seized.

The map was erased too. To collect tribute and police the faith, the colony gathered the scattered people of the calpultin into new towns, the congregaciones [16]. In Tututepec, the Otomí and Tepehua settlements went from 88 to 27 [16], and whoever went back to their plot could find it taken by a cattle ranch or a hacienda [16].

And the names. Cuauhnáhuac, «beside the forest», became Cuernavaca, «cow's horn» [52]. Valladolid was founded in 1541 on a Purépecha settlement; today it is called Morelia, and its own name never came back [53].

From 1607 on, the Desagüe of Huehuetoca began draining the lakes to protect the Spanish city, and it took away the livelihood of the towns that lived from the lake [54].

Whether Mexico should demand that Spain apologize is one debate. Knowing what happened is another, and in Mexico it is learned from primary school on: the SEP's history lesson for fourth grade teaches that the epidemics of the sixteenth century made the Indigenous population fall, that entire communities of Mesoamerica were emptied, and that new forms of labor exploitation arrived [5].

Even so, not even Mexico fully grasps it. The same lesson speaks of «a significant decline». Cook and Borah estimated 25.2 million people in central Mexico in 1518 and some 730,000 by 1620–1625 [4]: of every hundred, three remained. And what survived of their writing is far away: the Dresden Codex, in Saxony [55]; the Mendoza, in Oxford [9]; the Florentine, in Florence [10]. The quetzal-feather headdress is still in Vienna, which says it is too fragile to travel [56].

In Spain it is not usually part of what is taught. A 2026 study of three history textbooks for 2nd year of ESO, for students aged 13 and 14, found that the demographic and cultural annihilation of the Conquest stays hidden behind the story of an «encounter» between cultures and the advance of civilization [3]. In one of those books, slavery appears as a formality: people «brought» from Africa «where Indigenous labor was scarce» [3]. The openly civilizing story has been left behind, but slavery is still often omitted [3].

And some go further and call it the «Black Legend»: propaganda invented by the empire's rivals. The book Imperiofobia y leyenda negra (2016) sold more than 100,000 copies in Spain with a vindication of the Spanish empire; its critics point to quotes taken out of context, manipulated bibliography and omissions [57]. Among what the book does not address, writes Breny Mendoza, is the destruction of the Indigenous world [57].

That denial goes back a long way and carried an official seal. Francoism made the Catholic Monarchs, the “conquest” and the Empire milestones of its history, and the American past a “crowning glory” [58]. In 1940 it created the Consejo de la Hispanidad, the platform for the regime's propaganda in the Americas [58]. With democracy, the story went on. The 1987 law, signed by Juan Carlos I, set 12 October as the national holiday: on that day, it says, “a period of linguistic and cultural projection beyond the limits of Europe” begins [59]. In 2001 the same king said of Spanish: “Ours was never a language of imposition, but of encounter” [60].

Today that defense is political. “The books that whitewash the Spanish Empire are a suicide of intelligence,” the philosopher José Luis Villacañas said in 2019. To say that empire did everything right, he added, is to say that “you would do it again” [61]. In 2021, in Washington, Isabel Díaz Ayuso, president of the Community of Madrid, called indigenismos “the new communism” [62].

In Mexico, the word that covered the blow was a different one: mestizaje. The idea of mestizaje as national history and destiny arose under Porfirio Díaz, with Justo Sierra, and was carried on by Manuel Gamio, José Vasconcelos and Octavio Paz until it became the hegemonic definition [63]. A plaque says it in Tlatelolco: “It was neither triumph nor defeat. It was the painful birth of the mestizo people that is the Mexico of today” [64]. If nobody lost, nobody has anything to claim. But “Mexican mestizaje has always promoted the whitening of the Indians, not the Indianization of the whites,” writes the historian Federico Navarrete, who calls it a legend [63]. In 1987 Guillermo Bonfil Batalla spoke of a denied civilization: deep Mexico, against an imaginary Mexico that follows the Western model inherited from the Conquest [65].

Recognition came late. Not until 1992 did the Constitution say the nation has a “pluricultural composition originally based on Indigenous peoples” [66]: 471 years after the fall of Tenochtitlan. In 2021, in Guaymas, the president apologized to the Yaquis for “crimes of state” and offered to return 2,943 hectares [67]. Not until September 2024 did a reform of Article 2 recognize the peoples as “subjects of public law, with legal personality and their own assets” [68].

Beyond Mexico and Spain, what is missing is simpler: knowing where it came from. Chocolate is called Swiss or Belgian; vanilla, Madagascar or «Bourbon»; the tomato, Italian. The turkey is named as if it came from Turkey, and the poinsettia carries the name of a U.S. diplomat. The world uses what Mexico contributed every day without knowing it came from here. And whoever does not know what Mexico gave cannot see what it lost either.

Meanwhile, the Mexican state says it louder and louder, and Spain is only beginning to admit it. The second timeline below sets what is acknowledged against what is denied.

Denying it does not erase it, and neither does ignoring it. The codices, chronicles and figures on this journey are there for anyone who wants to see them.

What never made it into the count

No figure on this journey counts what cost the most.

The labor of millions of people was never paid. Lives have no price. The books that were burned cannot be bought back, and the names that were lost do not return on their own.

That is why this section is called what it is. Not because Mexico does not know it, but because elsewhere it is still denied, minimized or simply not told.
